# 33 The Garbage Can

*"And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you."* - [Ephesians 4:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/eph/4?lang=eng&id=p32#p32)

In the 1930s in Montevideo, when the garbage collector came by, the truck carried two or three sanitation workers on the back for the collection. In those days, the containers for trash were very varied: homemade cans made from paint tins, wooden crates in the humblest homes, and even reused remains of water heaters and washing machines.

Among the collectors there was a man who, after emptying the containers into the truck, would take "the cans" and send them flying through the air, hurling them violently against the walls of the houses. Such was that man's disregard for those humble containers that he seemed to unload all his anger on them for no reason at all.

When he reached a certain block, he was especially hard on a little wooden crate. It was simple and modest, but it was the one he attacked the hardest. Since the collector came by more than once a week, the same destructive story repeated itself every day.

The poor little crate went flying through the air again and again.

When my grandfather heard "the bang" of the little crate crashing against the walls of his house, he would patiently go out to gather up the remains of the mistreated container. Carefully he would take the shattered boards and join each piece again, one by one, until he had put it completely back together. It seemed that the same story repeated itself over and over: destruction, patience, and rebuilding.

One day, knowing beforehand when the collector would arrive, my grandfather waited with determination. He had a large hammer in his hand and said with indignation: "This man is going to learn. I hope I get him good and close, and he will get what he deserves."

When the dreaded moment came and the familiar "bang" was heard, my grandfather went out at once. He picked up his little crate, now in pieces, gripped the hammer tightly, and called the garbage collector to face him.

The worker came over, probably expecting an argument or an angry complaint. Yet what he saw left him completely disarmed: without saying a single word, my grandfather knelt down in the street, took the loose boards, pulled nails from his pocket, and began patiently to rebuild the little wooden crate, piece by piece.

The collector watched in silence that man kneeling on the pavement, working with care and dedication to repair what he himself had destroyed. The simplicity and humility of the scene struck him deeply. In that moment, something changed in his heart.

From that day on, that man completely changed his attitude. He changed the way he saw things, he changed his heart, and he changed the way he treated the crates and garbage cans. My grandfather did not need harsh words or revenge; with his example of patience and forgiveness, he transformed a hardened heart.

Sometimes the greatest teaching is given not with words, but with actions full of love and understanding.
