# 1 I Learned to Ride a Bicycle

"Behold, my Spirit rests upon you... walk with me" (Moses 6:34)

When I was a kid, the neighborhood children rode their bicycles on the sidewalk for safety—the street was too dangerous. I didn't know how to ride a bike and didn't have one, so I just watched as the others had fun with their bicycles. A neighbor, the father of one of the kids who was riding bikes and had just come home from work, noticed me and asked why I didn't join the other children to play with them. I answered sheepishly, "I don't know how to ride a bike." "Oh, but don't worry, I'm going to teach you," he said.

The next day he said, "We're going to El Prado" (which was 4 blocks away). "Come with us" (his family) "and bring the bikes," and my brother and I went with them. At the "Hotel del Prado," back then a barely-maintained restaurant, there was an old outdoor dance floor they weren't using that would be perfect for "learning to ride a bicycle."

Sergio, this kind neighbor, told me, "Come on, hop on," adjusted the seat height and gave me instructions on how to pedal and steer to make the bicycle "work." He whispered in my ear, "Alejandro, don't worry, you can do this, you can do this, you're going to make it!" He started running alongside me, at first holding the handlebars and pushing me from the bike seat. At one point he told me, "Pedal! Come on, come on, come on!" and I worked up the courage to pedal, and "good, good, good!" he cheered. After a while he told me, "You can control the bike—I'm letting go of the handlebars now," and I could steer—I was really controlling the bicycle. "Go, go, go! You can do it!"

He was still running with me, pushing the seat. "Keep going, keep going, keep going!" and I felt his breath and intense encouragement, but I sensed his voice getting farther and farther away, and my brother, who I saw nearby, was amazed and said, "You can ride a bike now!" And I said, "WHAT?!" I looked to the side and Sergio wasn't there—I was going fast, but what I still didn't know was how to brake! He hadn't explained that yet! When I looked back and saw that Sergio wasn't there, I lost focus and crashed into a tree. Fortunately, I wasn't going very fast, and I just got some scrapes. "Come on, come on, get up, that was nothing," Sergio said encouragingly. "I was just about to explain how the brakes work." That bicycle was very easy to brake because it had 3 brakes: front, rear, and also a "coaster" brake (if you pedal backward, the bike brakes). Well, I learned very well what happens when you don't brake, in a very practical and memorable way. We rode our bikes home after that wonderful experience and the effort of this neighbor-friend's father.

Life has its twists and turns, and several years passed as we all grew up, and one day I found out that Sergio had suffered a stroke at work. What sad news, but he was able to pull through, and shortly after they discharged him and sent him home. He had lost mobility on the left side of his body. I learned that they had installed pulleys and ropes so he could do rehabilitation exercises for physical therapy, which he was supposed to do every day. His children were studying and working, and there was no time to help Sergio with the exercises. His wife cooked and helped Sergio with the exercises when she could. One day I realized and said to myself, "This woman is overwhelmed, I have to do something." So I approached her and said, "Can I help you with Sergio?" Her face lit up. "Really?" she said. "I have some time before I start high school," I replied.

I went to see Sergio. There he was, kind of "shut down" and seemed like "a different person." I greeted him and I don't think he recognized me. People change—"kids grow up, he's Teresa's son, do you remember him?" his wife told him. He had a vacant stare and barely spoke. I told him, "Don't worry, I'm here to help you." I put his arm that he couldn't move in a sling, and with the other he had to strain to lift it—it was a simple exercise but very difficult given his condition. I started doing exercises with him, encouraging him with each one. At one point I leaned close to his ear, whispering, and said, "Sergio, don't worry, you can do this, you can do this, you're going to make it!" At that moment his eyes came alive as if they lit up, and then a tear rolled down his cheek. He remembered that magical moment at El Prado, but this time he was receiving the gift that he had once given me. Sergio and I did the exercises until I no longer had time to spend with him. He had recovered some of his mobility.

This experience taught me that when we give love, it comes back multiplied.
